One control variable: battery power

Speed is the wrong thing to hold on a solar boat — it commits you to a power draw the sun may not deliver. SolarHelm holds battery power instead. The shunt measures net battery flow at one point of truth; the controller trims the motor until that flow matches the target. Target 0 W: propulsion consumes exactly what the panels produce after hotel loads, and speed floats with the sun. Target −200 W: the boat is allowed to draw 200 W from the pack for more speed. Target +500 W: the boat must keep charging at 500 W while moving.

This one choice makes the whole system self-balancing. Clouds cut PV by 80%? Battery power goes negative, the controller ramps the motor down, balance returns — no forecast, no PV model, no MPPT coupling needed in the loop. The physics does the accounting; the controller only follows it.

The control pipeline

 measured battery power (shunt, ~1 Hz)
   │
   ▼
 low-pass filter (τ = 2 s)      – waves and PV ripple don't reach the prop
   ▼
 deadband (±25 W, FREEZES cmd)  – no hunting around the target
   ▼
 PI controller                  – clamped integrator + tracking anti-windup
   ▼
 slew limiter (+2 / −5 %/s)     – gentle up, decisive down
   ▼
 clamp [0..100 %], envelope ceiling ──► throttle DAC ──► motor controller

Each stage exists because a failure mode demands it. The filter stops wave-induced power ripple from being amplified into throttle ripple. The deadband freezes the command instead of zeroing the error — zeroing would collapse the proportional term and sag the command on entry. The integrator is re-seated against the rate-limited output, so a fast solar rise produces zero overshoot instead of the ~4 percentage points plain PI gives. Down-slew is 2.5× faster than up-slew because losing sun is a safety event and gaining sun is not. All of this is demonstrated with committed graphs in the simulation results: battery power held at ≈0 W mean through 300–1500 W solar swings.

Modes

MANUAL
The default, in hardware. The relay is normally closed on the helm potentiometer; SolarHelm unpowered means the boat behaves as if it were never installed.
SOLAR
Target 0 W. Pure solar cruising — this is the solar-equilibrium speed, found by feedback instead of solved from a model.
SOLAR+
Negative target (e.g. −200 W): a bounded battery contribution for more speed. A positive target gives surplus-charging cruise.
REMOTE
The phone planner streams a motor power target. Ten seconds without a fresh target and the boat degrades to SOLAR — safety never depends on the phone.
Reserve floor
At the reserve SOC the target is floored so the pack cannot be drawn down further, with hysteresis so it doesn't chatter at the boundary.
Fail-safe exits
Stale shunt data, an unhealthy DAC, a watchdog reset — every fault path ends at MANUAL with the automatic throttle at zero.

The protection envelope

Underneath the mode logic, a guard enforces the hardware's limits regardless of what any controller wants: staged voltage-sag backoff (soft sag caps the command at 50%, hard sag at 15%, deep sag forces MANUAL — each stage debounced and released with hysteresis), a discharge-current cap below the BMS rating, and temperature derating with a hard stop. Frequent small power adjustments do not wear the drivetrain — every aging mechanism keys on averages, amplitudes, heat and reversals, not on the count of gentle rate-limited changes (the research). Holding battery power near zero actually extends LiFePO4 life by minimizing throughput.

Zoom out: the day is the unit of energy

 daily solar harvest
   + battery buffer
   − propulsion energy
   − hotel energy
   ─────────────────────
   = daily energy balance     (≥ 0 → you can cruise like this forever)

For a solar cruiser the battery is a buffer — it bridges clouds, mornings and maneuvering — not a fuel tank. The planner therefore optimizes daily balance, not battery range: the voyage optimizer sweeps departure times and power settings against the solar/wind/wave forecast, keeps the reserve by construction, and reports arrival state-of-charge with calibrated uncertainty. Real voyages feed telemetry back into the boat's learned efficiency model, so every trip makes the next prediction better. The Helios 11 case study is the real-world proof this regime works: ~3,000 nautical miles on a ≤30 kWh pack, powered by the day, buffered by the battery.

Boundaries. The phone plans; the boat executes and protects. The controller never optimizes energy at the expense of control authority, the skipper outranks everything, and SolarHelm is not a certified navigation system.
